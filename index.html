Why a higher rate can still come out close

Interest is rate × balance × time. A HELOC or all-in-one loan charges its rate only on what you owe that day. When your paycheck lands in the account, it lowers that balance right away, until the money goes back out for bills. That's a real saving, but it's small. The speed itself comes from your leftover cash, which extra payments use just as well. A HELOC or all-in-one loan changes how that cash is routed, which is why the difference is usually small.

If things change

Risks to understand first

  • Your home secures all of these loans. Falling behind on a HELOC or all-in-one loan can lead to foreclosure, just like a mortgage.
  • The rate moves. HELOC and all-in-one rates are variable and follow the prime rate. A fixed mortgage rate never changes.
  • The line can be frozen or cut. Lenders may freeze or reduce a HELOC if your home's value drops a lot or your finances change. They must tell you within 3 business days after, not before.
  • It only works if spending stays below pay, every month. Available credit is easy to spend, and spending it undoes the plan. With a credit card, one missed full payment costs more than the float saves.
  • Setup is harder than it sounds. Most HELOCs can't receive direct deposit or pay bills directly; pay usually lands in checking and gets moved over. In Texas, state law sets a $4,000 minimum per HELOC draw and bans card access, which makes chunking impractical there. All-in-one loans work differently, so ask the lender what's available in your state.
  • Draw periods end. Many HELOCs let you borrow for about 10 years. After that, the line stops working as an account and required payments jump.
  • Extra payments have a catch too. Money you prepay is locked in the house; you can't get it back without selling or borrowing. Keep an emergency fund before prepaying anything.

How each method works

Current schedule: you pay the required payment. Leftover money stays in your bank account.

Extra payments: every month you send your leftover money to the mortgage as extra principal. Simple, fixed-rate, no new loan. The money can't be taken back out.

HELOC (“velocity banking” or “chunking”): you open a home equity line of credit next to your mortgage. You borrow a lump sum (a “chunk”) from it and pay it onto the mortgage principal. Your pay goes into the HELOC, bills come out of it, and the leftover pays the HELOC back down. When it reaches $0, you move the next chunk. You still make your regular mortgage payment.

All-in-one loan: a first-lien HELOC replaces your mortgage entirely and doubles as your bank account. All income goes in, all spending comes out, and interest is charged daily on whatever you owe. There's no separate mortgage payment. Few lenders offer these.

If the HELOC rate is higher, how can it ever save money?

On its own, it can't. Move $20,000 from a 6.5% mortgage to an 8.5% HELOC and change nothing else, and you pay more. Any savings come from your pay sitting against the balance for a few days or weeks before bills go out. If that saves more than the extra rate costs, the HELOC comes out ahead. If not, it comes out behind. The breakdown above shows both numbers for you.

So what's the real benefit of a HELOC or all-in-one loan?

Access to your money. With extra payments, cash you've prepaid is stuck in the house. With a HELOC or all-in-one loan, you can borrow it back if something goes wrong, as long as the line stays open. Whether that flexibility is worth the extra rate, fees and risk is your call. It isn't a dollar amount this tool can put on it.

What if my HELOC or all-in-one rate is lower than my mortgage rate?

Then the method can genuinely beat extra payments, because every borrowed dollar costs less than the mortgage dollar it replaces. This mostly happens with mortgages taken out at high rates. It's also the situation where refinancing into a lower fixed rate is worth pricing, since that savings is locked in.

My mortgage rate is low. Should I pay it off early at all?

Every extra dollar toward the mortgage earns exactly your mortgage rate, guaranteed. If your savings or investments reliably earn more than that, paying off early may not be the best use of the money. Investments carry risk, though, while mortgage payoff doesn't. This tool doesn't compare investing; it only compares ways to pay the mortgage.

Is HELOC interest tax-deductible?

Under current federal rules, home equity interest is generally deductible only when the borrowed money is used to buy, build, or substantially improve the home securing the loan, and only if you itemize. Whether paying down a mortgage with a HELOC qualifies depends on your situation. Ask a tax professional; this tool ignores taxes.

What about recasting or refinancing to a shorter loan?

A recast lowers your required payment after a large principal payment, but it doesn't save interest by itself. The savings came from the principal you paid. Refinancing to a 15- or 20-year loan usually gets a lower fixed rate and a guaranteed payoff date, but costs closing fees and raises the required payment. Both are worth asking a lender about alongside these options.

Questions to ask a lender before opening a HELOC or all-in-one loan
  • Is interest charged daily on the average daily balance? Is the year 365 or 360 days?
  • Can my paycheck be deposited directly, and do deposits lower the balance the same day?
  • Can I pay bills directly from the line, or only through a linked checking account?
  • What's the rate today, what's the margin over prime, and is there a cap or floor?
  • What are the fees: opening, annual, per draw, inactivity, early closure?
  • How long is the draw period, and what happens to payments when it ends?
  • Under what conditions can you freeze or reduce my line?
  • What's the maximum loan-to-value, and what credit score do you require?
How this calculator works and what it assumes
  • All four methods use the same pay, the same spending and the same starting balance, so the only difference is how the money is routed.
  • Mortgage interest is charged monthly. HELOC and all-in-one interest is charged daily on the day's balance (rate ÷ 365) and added to the balance monthly, using real calendar months.
  • Pay arrives on the schedule you pick. Spending leaves evenly through the month, or on the 25th if you choose the credit card option. The mortgage payment leaves on the 1st.
  • The HELOC starts by moving one chunk onto the mortgage. After that, a new chunk moves each time the HELOC is paid back to $0, up to your credit limit. The limit is fixed when the line opens; it doesn't grow as your home appreciates.
  • Rates stay the same for the whole loan unless you change them. The “If things change” table shows what happens if they rise.
  • Taxes, investment returns, home appreciation, inflation and any change in pay over time are left out.
  • Compared with one lender's own all-in-one simulator, this tool came out a little more optimistic: 90 vs. 92 payments on the same inputs. The difference is how much credit each gives for pay sitting against the balance.
  • Your numbers stay in your browser. Nothing is sent anywhere.
